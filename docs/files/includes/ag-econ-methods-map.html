Model versus estimation

A structural paper makes four separate choices, and students often treat them as one. "We estimate a BLP model" names a model and, loosely, an estimator. It does not say what variation identifies the parameters. Separate the four layers and each one can be evaluated on its own terms.

1. ModelWhat generates the data?

The economic primitives: preferences, technology, information, and how agents interact (equilibrium). The model defines the parameters and what a counterfactual means.

BLP: consumer i's utility from product j depends on price and attributes, with tastes that vary across consumers, plus a product-level demand shock ξ the researcher does not observe. Firms may also set prices in a Bertrand-Nash equilibrium.

Common mistake: treating the functional form as a detail. In logit, the functional form alone determines substitution patterns.

2. IdentificationWhat variation pins down the parameters?

The argument that, with infinite data, only one set of parameter values is consistent with what you observe. This is independent of the estimator, and it is where reduced-form thinking enters structural work.

BLP: price is correlated with ξ, because firms see the demand shock when they set prices. Identification needs instruments: cost shifters, characteristics of rival products, or prices of the same product in other markets. The taste dispersion is identified by how substitution changes as the choice set changes.

Common mistake: answering "how is it identified?" with "by GMM." GMM is an estimator. Identification is about the instruments and the variation.

3. EstimationWhat objective, and how do you find its optimum?

An estimator, which is an objective function built from the data, plus an algorithm to optimize it. Some estimators have a closed form (OLS, 2SLS). Most structural estimators require numerical search.

BLP: generalized method of moments on the moment condition E[ξ·Z] = 0, where Z is the instruments. Because ξ has no closed form, the algorithm nests a fixed-point calculation inside the optimization (see the steps below).

Common mistake: reporting estimates without checking whether the optimizer found the global optimum.

4. Inference and outputHow uncertain, and what do you report?

Standard errors for the parameters, then the objects the paper is actually about: elasticities, markups, welfare, and counterfactuals. These are functions of the parameters, so their uncertainty comes from the delta method, the bootstrap, or simulation.

BLP: clustered GMM standard errors for the parameters; elasticities and merger simulations computed from the estimates, with standard errors for those too.

Common mistake: reporting standard errors on parameters but not on the welfare number in the abstract.

BLP, step by step

BLP is estimated by GMM, and nobody should hand-code it for a dissertation. The standard tool is PyBLP (Conlon and Gortmaker 2020), which is in Python and can be called from R through reticulate. Teach it as a sequence in which each step adds one complication.

  1. Plain logit is a linear IV regression. With market shares, ln(sj/s0) = xβ − αp + ξ (Berry 1994). Estimate by 2SLS with instruments for price. Students can run this today with fixest. The problem is the substitution pattern: every product loses share in proportion to its size.
  2. Nested logit is still linear IV. Add ln(share within nest) as a regressor, which also needs an instrument. Substitution is now stronger within nests.
  3. Random coefficients break linearity. Tastes for price and attributes now vary with dispersion σ. Shares no longer invert analytically, so for any guess of σ the mean utilities δ have to be solved for.
  4. Inner loop. For a given σ, simulate a set of consumers and solve for the δ that makes predicted shares equal observed shares. This uses a contraction mapping, a fixed-point iteration that is guaranteed to converge. Use a tight tolerance, or the outer loop optimizes noise.
  5. Linear parameters by IV. Regress δ on x and p with instruments to recover β and α. The residual is ξ(σ).
  6. Outer loop. Search over σ to minimize the GMM objective ξ(σ)′Z W Z′ξ(σ). This is the nested fixed point. An alternative formulation, MPEC, solves the same problem as one constrained optimization (Dubé, Fox and Su 2012).
  7. Check and report. Use several starting values, check that the gradient is near zero at the optimum, and report elasticities and diversion ratios. Results can be sensitive to optimizer choices (Knittel and Metaxoglou 2014). Add supply-side moments or micro moments from household data when the aggregate shares alone identify σ poorly.

Grid search, optimizers, and nested loops

Grid search evaluates the objective at every point on a grid of parameter values and keeps the best. It is transparent and cannot get stuck at a local optimum within the grid, but the cost grows exponentially: 100 points per parameter means 10,000 evaluations for two parameters and 1010 for five. Use it for one or two nonlinear parameters (a temperature threshold, a discount factor, a kink location), for starting values, and for plotting the objective to see whether it is well behaved.

Gradient-based optimizers (BFGS, Newton, Nelder-Mead without gradients) move uphill from a starting point. They are fast and scale to many parameters, but find a local optimum. Multiple starting values are the basic safeguard.

Concentrating out parameters that enter linearly, as in step 5 above, means the numerical search covers only the nonlinear parameters. This is what makes many structural models feasible.

Nested fixed point refers to any estimation where evaluating the objective requires solving the model: inverting shares in BLP, solving the value function in dynamic discrete choice or option value models, or solving for prices in a spatial equilibrium. The inner solution must be accurate, and the outer search is only as good as the inner one.

Recovery tests are the most useful habit to teach. Simulate data from the model with parameters you choose, run your estimator, and check that it returns them. If it cannot recover planted parameters, it will not recover real ones.

Class exercise: recover a parameter you planted

Fifteen minutes in R. Students simulate purchase decisions with a known price coefficient, then recover it three ways: grid search, a numerical optimizer, and the canned function. The point is to see that glm runs the second method for them.

set.seed(570)
n     <- 2000
price <- runif(n, 1, 5)
beta  <- c(2, -0.8)                      # planted intercept and price coefficient
buy   <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(beta[1] + beta[2] * price))

loglik <- function(b) {
  p <- plogis(b[1] + b[2] * price)
  sum(buy * log(p) + (1 - buy) * log(1 - p))
}

# 1. Grid search over the price coefficient, intercept held at its true value
grid <- seq(-2, 0, by = 0.01)
ll   <- sapply(grid, function(b2) loglik(c(2, b2)))
grid[which.max(ll)]
plot(grid, ll, type = "l")               # look at the shape of the objective

# 2. Numerical optimizer over both parameters, from a poor starting value
fit <- optim(c(0, 0), function(b) -loglik(b), method = "BFGS", hessian = TRUE)
fit$par
sqrt(diag(solve(fit$hessian)))           # standard errors from the Hessian

# 3. The canned estimator does step 2 for you
coef(glm(buy ~ price, family = binomial))

# 4. A quantity of interest: price elasticity at mean price, by delta method or bootstrap

Discussion: Why hold the intercept fixed in the grid search? What would a grid over both parameters cost, and over ten? What does the plotted log-likelihood suggest about whether the optimizer can get lost here, and why might BLP be different?

Example: climate and U.S. agriculture

How will a warmer climate affect U.S. agriculture? Three designs answer this question using different comparisons, so each one estimates a different relationship.

Ricardian model

Regression

Farmland value per acre on long-run average temperature and precipitation, across counties (Mendelsohn, Nordhaus, and Shaw 1994).

What it estimates

The long-run effect of climate on land values, including the adaptation farmers have already made.

Identifying assumption

No omitted factor varies with climate across counties and affects land values. Irrigation violates this assumption (Schlenker, Hanemann, and Fisher 2005).

Panel weather model

Regression

County crop yields or farm profits on year-to-year weather, with county and year fixed effects (Deschênes and Greenstone 2007; Schlenker and Roberts 2009).

What it estimates

The short-run response to weather, including yield losses above crop-specific temperature thresholds.

Identifying assumption

Within a county, year-to-year deviations in weather are as good as random. Farmers cannot fully adapt to a single hot year, so the estimate may overstate long-run damage.

Long differences

Regression

Changes in county outcomes over several decades on changes in climate over the same period (Burke and Emerick 2016).

What it estimates

The response to gradual climate change. Compared with the panel estimate, it shows how much adaptation offsets short-run damage.

Identifying assumption

Within a state, counties' climate trends are unrelated to other trends in their outcomes.

The three answers differ because the designs estimate different relationships, not only because one design is more credible than another. Timmins and Schlenker (2009) discuss this tradeoff between cross-sectional and panel variation.
