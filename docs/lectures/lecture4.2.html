Today’s task

Turn an economic mechanism into a section a reader can follow.

  • State the role of theory in the paper.
  • Define the model’s elements before combining them.
  • Derive and interpret the relevant prediction.
  • Explain the bridge from theory to empirical evidence.

Theory in theoretical and empirical papers

Theoretical contribution Empirical contribution
Develop or modify a model Explain the mechanism behind the empirical question
Establish analytical results Derive the hypotheses to be evaluated
Show what changes relative to existing theory Connect theoretical concepts to measured variables

Both require explicit assumptions and a coherent argument.

Source: Chouinard (2015).

Build the argument in order

  1. Explain the relationship to existing theory.
  2. Define agents, choices, and the feasible set.
  3. State assumptions.
  4. Present the objective or relevant relationships.
  5. Explain the derivation.
  6. Interpret the result and connect it to the question.

Avoid making readers infer a missing step.

Definitions, assumptions, and results

  • A definition tells readers what a term means.
  • An assumption specifies what the argument takes as given.
  • A proposition states a result established from those premises.
  • A corollary follows from an established result.

Labeling a claim “Proposition” does not establish it.

Source: Chouinard (2015).

Make equations readable

  • Introduce each symbol.
  • State the domain and units when relevant.
  • Explain what changes and what stays fixed.
  • Follow a mathematical result with its economic interpretation.
  • Put lengthy derivations in an appendix when they interrupt the argument.

From a prediction to a measurement

Return to the irrigation example.

Model concept Possible empirical measure Question to resolve
Water price Marginal charge per unit Is there a fixed fee or tiered rate?
Water use Metered withdrawals Do withdrawals equal crop consumption?
Technology Irrigation system Can adoption change with the price?

A theoretical variable and its proxy are rarely interchangeable without explanation.

Identification asks a different question

The framework explains why a relationship might exist.

Identification asks what evidence distinguishes that relationship from competing explanations.

For water prices and use:

  • Do more water-scarce places set higher prices?
  • Do weather and prices move together?
  • Do farmers select into different pricing arrangements?

Common threats

  • Omitted variables: another factor affects both the explanatory variable and outcome.
  • Reverse causality: the outcome helps determine the explanatory variable.
  • Selection: compared groups differ for reasons related to the outcome.

Naming a regression method does not resolve these threats.

Workshop: write the bridge

Draft one paragraph with this structure:

  1. “The model predicts …”
  2. “We measure this concept using …”
  3. “We use variation in …”
  4. “Interpreting this variation causally requires …”
  5. “The main competing explanation is …”

Peer review: can you reconstruct the argument?

Read a partner’s paragraph without additional explanation.

  • Underline the prediction.
  • Circle the assumption carrying the causal interpretation.
  • Identify one undefined concept.
  • Suggest one revision that makes the logic easier to follow.

Before you leave

Identify one change to your framework and one piece of evidence you need before selecting an empirical strategy.
