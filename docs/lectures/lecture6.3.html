Lectures 6.1 and 6.2 estimated parameters; machine learning targets prediction

  • The designs in 6.1 and the models in 6.2 recover parameters: a treatment effect, a price coefficient, an elasticity
  • Supervised machine learning addresses a different objective: the most accurate prediction of yy given xx for observations outside the estimation sample
  • In Mullainathan and Spiess’s words, machine learning “not only provides new tools, it solves a different problem”
  • This lecture covers where these methods came from, the three roles they play in empirical economics, and how a methods section reports them

Source: Mullainathan and Spiess (2017), pages 87 to 88.

  • Frame the lecture around the distinction between estimating a parameter and predicting an outcome. Every later slide returns to it.
  • Transition: these methods developed largely outside economics, which explains much about how economists use them.

Background

Machine learning entered applied economics within the last decade

Period Development
1984 to 2001 Regression trees (Breiman et al., 1984), the LASSO (Tibshirani, 1996), and random forests (Breiman, 2001)
1990s Neural networks studied in econometrics, but they “did not catch on at the time”
2001 Breiman’s “two cultures”: statistics relied on data models, while an algorithmic culture grew in other fields
2014 Early reviews for economists: Varian; Einav and Levin; Belloni, Chernozhukov, and Hansen
2017 to 2019 Mullainathan and Spiess; causal forests (Wager and Athey); Athey and Imbens’s review for economists
2020 to 2025 Reviews and special issues in agricultural and applied economics: Storm et al. (2020); Maruejols et al. (2025)

Sources: Athey and Imbens (2019), pages 686 to 688, 696 to 698, and 701; Mullainathan and Spiess (2017), page 87; Storm et al. (2020); Maruejols et al. (2025).

  • The methods themselves are older; their wide use in applied economics dates from roughly the mid-2010s.
  • Athey and Imbens open with Breiman (2001): one culture “assumes that the data are generated by a given stochastic data model”; the other “uses algorithmic models and treats the data mechanism as unknown.” Econometrics belongs to the first culture.
  • Statistics has since absorbed the algorithmic approach; Athey and Imbens note that “the adoption of these methods in economics has been slower.”
  • The dates for the original methods come from the citations in Athey and Imbens.
  • Transition: why adoption was slow, and why it accelerated.

Why economists adopted machine learning, and why adoption was slow

  • New data: many units, many variables per unit, and unstructured sources such as satellite images and text
  • Prediction questions: some policy problems require an accurate forecast and no causal parameter
  • Many candidate variables: the analyst must choose controls or functional forms from a large set
  • Why adoption was slow: economics journals emphasize formal properties of estimators, such as consistency and asymptotic normality, which early machine learning methods did not provide
  • What changed: recent work established inference for adapted methods, including double selection and causal forests

Sources: Athey and Imbens (2019), pages 686 to 687; Mullainathan and Spiess (2017), pages 88, 98, and 102.

  • Athey and Imbens: machine learning research focuses on “working properties of algorithms in specific settings,” with formal results such as error rates, while econometrics focuses on large-sample properties of estimators and tests.
  • The methods economists now use are often adaptations: they modify machine learning algorithms to deliver valid confidence intervals for causal parameters. Two appear later in this lecture.
  • Transition: how prediction and estimation are evaluated.

Prediction and parameter estimation are evaluated by different criteria

Prediction, ŷ\hat{y} Parameter estimation, β̂\hat{\beta}
Target The outcome for a new observation The effect of a change in xx on yy
Evaluation Accuracy on data not used in estimation Identification: the source of variation and its assumptions
Verifiability Directly testable: predictions and outcomes are both observed Rests on assumptions about the data-generating process
Typical methods LASSO, random forests, neural networks OLS, IV, difference in differences, structural models

Source: Mullainathan and Spiess (2017), pages 88 and 92.

  • The verifiability row is the central contrast. Prediction quality can be measured against held-out outcomes; a causal effect cannot, because the counterfactual is never observed.
  • This is why machine learning papers report out-of-sample performance and causal papers report an identification strategy.
  • Transition: three ways economics papers combine the two.

Machine learning plays three roles in empirical economics

Role What machine learning does Example
1. Prediction as the objective Predicts an outcome, such as household food insecurity Zhao et al. (2025)
2. Prediction within a larger analysis Selects the variables that enter an economic model or a treatment-effect regression Li and Yu (2025); Belloni et al. (2014)
3. Heterogeneous treatment effects Estimates how a treatment effect varies across farms or households Stetter et al. (2022)

In roles 2 and 3, identification still comes from the research design. Machine learning does not supply exogenous variation.

Sources: Mullainathan and Spiess (2017); Athey and Imbens (2019), pages 686 to 688; Maruejols et al. (2025).

  • This table organizes the rest of the lecture; each role has its own section.
  • Roles 2 and 3 connect directly to 6.1 and 6.2: the machine learning step sits inside a causal or structural analysis.
  • The final sentence is the main point of the lecture.
  • Transition: two concepts needed to read any of these papers.

Core concepts

Out-of-sample performance: training data, test data, and cross-validation

  • A flexible model can fit noise in its estimation sample, so in-sample fit overstates predictive performance (overfitting)
  • Training and test data: the model is estimated on one subset and evaluated on a held-out subset
  • Cross-validation: the training data are divided into KK folds; the model is estimated on K−1K-1 folds and evaluated on the remaining fold, in rotation, to select tuning parameters
Paper Sample split
Zhao et al. (2025) 70% training (756 households), 30% test (324 households)
Li and Yu (2025) 10-fold cross-validation to select the LASSO penalty

Sources: Mullainathan and Spiess (2017), pages 89 to 92; Bittmann (2025), Section 3.

  • Mullainathan and Spiess describe cross-validation as creating “an out-of-sample experiment inside the original sample.”
  • The test set is used once, for the final evaluation. If it informs model choices, it no longer measures out-of-sample performance.
  • With panel or spatial data, the split should respect that structure, for example by training on earlier years and testing on later ones.
  • Transition: the two methods used in the example papers.

Regularization: the LASSO and random forests

LASSO adds a penalty on the absolute size of the coefficients to the least squares objective:

minβ∑i=1n(yi−xi′β)2+λ∑j=1p|βj| \min_{\beta} \; \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(y_i - x_i'\beta\right)^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} |\beta_j|

  • With λ=0\lambda = 0, the estimator is OLS; as λ\lambda increases, coefficients shrink and some are set exactly to zero
  • The LASSO therefore performs variable selection; λ\lambda is chosen by cross-validation

Random forests average the predictions of many regression trees, each estimated on a random subsample, which captures nonlinearities and interactions without specifying them in advance.

Sources: Mullainathan and Spiess (2017), pages 90 to 97; Athey and Imbens (2019), pages 690 and 695 to 699; Bittmann (2025), Section 4.

  • Regularization trades a small increase in bias for a larger reduction in variance, which improves out-of-sample prediction.
  • Athey and Imbens note that ridge regression and the LASSO have antecedents in econometrics and statistics; the penalty is the shared idea.
  • Transition: the first role, where prediction is the objective.

Role 1: Prediction as the objective

Zhao et al. (2025) predict food insecurity among rural households in China

  • Objective: classify rural households as calorie poor
  • Data: 1,080 households in Jilin, Shandong, and Hubei; 3-day food consumption records; 61.1% of households are calorie poor
  • Methods: random forest and LASSO, compared with a logit benchmark; 70% training and 30% test samples
Model (test sample) Accuracy
Random forest 65.7%
LASSO 61.1%
Logit benchmark 58.9%
Classify every household as calorie poor 61.1%

Accuracy is informative only relative to a benchmark; the final row is the majority-class rate.

Source: Zhao et al. (2025), Sections 3 to 5 and Table 3. The final row is our calculation from the sample share.

  • The paper also reports sensitivity, specificity, and the area under the ROC curve (0.62 for both the forest and the LASSO, against 0.5 for random classification).
  • The final row shows why benchmarks matter: assigning every household to the majority class is correct 61.1% of the time, so the forest improves on that rule by about 5 percentage points.
  • The authors address the class imbalance by oversampling the minority class (SMOTE) and recommend choosing the classification cut-off according to a program’s coverage.
  • Transition: what a prediction paper can conclude about the determinants of food insecurity.

Predictive importance does not identify causal effects

  • Zhao et al. rank predictors; household income and access to food markets rank highest in both models
  • A variable may predict well because it is correlated with an unobserved determinant of the outcome
  • LASSO selections are unstable: across ten random partitions of the same data, Mullainathan and Spiess find that the selected variables change from one partition to the next
  • Stetter et al. caution that their variable importance measure “should not be interpreted in a causal fashion”

A causal claim about income would require an identification strategy of the kind discussed in Lecture 6.1.

Sources: Zhao et al. (2025), Section 5.2; Mullainathan and Spiess (2017), page 97, Figure 2; Stetter et al. (2022), page 740, footnote 17.

  • Zhao et al.’s discussion moves from variables that predict food insecurity to variables that “significantly influence” it. That shift is where a reader should ask for an identification strategy.
  • The instability arises because correlated predictors are close substitutes in prediction; small changes in the sample change which one the LASSO retains.
  • Transition: the second role, where prediction is one step in a larger analysis.

Role 2: Prediction within a larger analysis

Li and Yu (2025) use the LASSO to select attributes before estimating willingness to pay

  • Setting: a choice experiment with 100 consumers in Göttingen; apples vary in origin, size, production method, and price
  • First stage, LASSO logit: 10-fold cross-validation selects λ\lambda, with price exempt from the penalty; the LASSO excludes Organic and Japan, interpreted as attribute non-attendance
  • Second stage, conditional logit: the random utility model from Lecture 6.2, with willingness to pay =−βk/βprice= -\beta_k / \beta_{\text{price}}
Willingness to pay (€ per kg), relative to China All attributes After LASSO
France 1.20 1.53
Italy 1.42 1.40
Small apples 1.30 1.41
Organic −0.04 excluded

Source: Li and Yu (2025), Sections 2 and 3, Tables 4, 6, and 7.

  • Here machine learning operates inside a structural model: the LASSO determines which attributes enter the utility function of the logit model from 6.2.
  • The economic estimates change: the premium for French apples rises from €1.20 to €1.53 per kilogram.
  • A question for discussion: the LASSO excludes Organic for the whole sample, so the paper cannot report willingness to pay for organic apples, the attribute most relevant to policy. Is that trade-off appropriate?
  • Transition: the same logic applied to the choice of control variables.

Belloni, Chernozhukov, and Hansen (2014) use the LASSO to select control variables

With a treatment did_i and many candidate controls xix_i, double selection proceeds in three steps:

  1. LASSO of the treatment did_i on xix_i, retaining controls that predict treatment
  2. LASSO of the outcome yiy_i on xix_i, retaining controls that predict the outcome
  3. OLS of yiy_i on did_i and the union of the two sets of controls
  • Selecting controls only for their ability to predict yy can omit a confounder that strongly predicts treatment
  • Application: abortion and crime; 48 states over 12 years (576 observations), 251 candidate controls, of which the LASSO retains 8 to 14
  • Identification: the estimate is causal only if treatment is as good as randomly assigned conditional on the controls

Source: Belloni et al. (2014), Sections 1, 2.2, and 7.

  • The first step is the methodological contribution. A confounder that predicts treatment strongly and the outcome weakly would be dropped by a LASSO for yy alone, producing omitted variable bias.
  • Their results: the property crime estimate remains close to the original study but is significant only at the 10% level; the violent crime and murder estimates become imprecise.
  • The identifying assumption is selection on observables, as in 6.1. The LASSO makes the choice of controls systematic and reproducible; it does not make the assumption more plausible.
  • Transition: the third role.

Role 3: Heterogeneous treatment effects

Stetter et al. (2022) estimate farm-level effects of agri-environment schemes with a causal forest

The estimand is the conditional average treatment effect:

τ(x)=E[Yi(1)−Yi(0)∣Xi=x] \tau(x) = E\left[\,Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) \mid X_i = x\,\right]

  • Data: 2,758 Bavarian farms in 2014, of which 1,641 participated in an agri-environment scheme; more than 130 farm and county characteristics
  • Method: forests estimate participation probabilities and expected outcomes; a causal forest then estimates τ(x)\tau(x) for each farm, with tuning parameters chosen by cross-validation
  • Results: fertiliser expenditure falls significantly for 33% of farms (by €14 per hectare among them); land-use diversity rises for 55%; effects on pesticide use and greenhouse gas emissions are rarely significant

Source: Stetter et al. (2022), Sections 3 to 5 and Table 3.

  • Yi(1)Y_i(1) and Yi(0)Y_i(0) are the potential outcomes from 6.1: a farm’s outcome with and without participation.
  • The forest identifies which characteristics are associated with larger or smaller effects; the authors use this to argue for targeting payments by location, farm size, and yield potential.
  • €14 per hectare corresponds to about 13 kg of nitrogen per hectare at the paper’s 2014 price.
  • Transition: the source of identification.

Causal forests rely on the same identifying assumptions as the designs in Lecture 6.1

  • Selection on observables: conditional on farm characteristics, participation is as good as randomly assigned
  • Overlap: every farm has a positive probability of each treatment status; estimated propensities range from 0.27 to 0.86, and 23 farms without overlap are excluded
  • The authors’ argument: a large set of characteristics allows the forest to proxy for unobserved factors, such as a farmer’s environmental attitude
  • The acknowledged limit: if a distinct group of confounders is absent from the data, “our estimates might still be biased”
  • Sensitivity analysis: placebo tests and simulated confounders

Source: Stetter et al. (2022), Sections 4.3 and 5.1.

  • In contrast to Zhao et al., the authors state the identifying assumption and test the sensitivity of their estimates to it. That is what makes the analysis causal.
  • The argument that many observed characteristics proxy for unobserved ones is itself an assumption, to be evaluated as any selection-on-observables design from 6.1 would be.
  • Transition: what a methods section must report in all three roles.

Reporting a machine learning method

What a methods section reports when it uses machine learning

Item Example
1. The role: prediction, a step in a larger analysis, or heterogeneous effects Zhao et al.: prediction is the objective
2. The target, the inputs, and a benchmark model Calorie poverty; household and market characteristics; logit
3. The sample split 70% training, 30% test
4. The choice of tuning parameters Cross-validation; number of trees
5. Out-of-sample performance Accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, area under the ROC curve
6. For roles 2 and 3: the identifying assumption and its checks Stetter et al.: selection on observables, overlap, placebo tests
7. Software and versions Li and Yu: the glmnet package in R

Sources: Zhao et al. (2025); Li and Yu (2025); Stetter et al. (2022); Athey and Imbens (2019), pages 686 to 687.

  • Items 1 to 5 apply to every paper that uses machine learning. Item 6 distinguishes a causal claim from a prediction.
  • Athey and Imbens: researchers “should articulate clearly the goals of their analysis and why certain properties of algorithms and estimators may or may not be important.” Item 1 does that.
  • Item 7 matters for replication, as in Unit 5: results depend on random sample splits, so report the software, versions, and random seed.
  • Transition: questions for the proposal.

What to consider for your proposal

  • The role: does the question concern predicting an outcome, selecting variables, or how an effect varies?
  • The claim: does the conclusion require a causal effect? If so, what is the design, and where does machine learning enter it?
  • The benchmark: which simpler model or rule should the method outperform?
  • The evidence: which out-of-sample measure, or which identification check, will the paper report?
  • Most proposals in this course will not use machine learning, but every student should be able to locate items 1 to 7 in a paper that does.
  • Students who use these methods should state the role in the first sentence of the methods section.
