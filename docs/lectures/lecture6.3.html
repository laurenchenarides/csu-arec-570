Machine learning in a nutshell

If you take away one idea today: ML is a prediction method learns from observations we already have, then we check how well it predicts observations it has not seen

  1. Choose the outcome to predict and the information available when a prediction is made
  2. Fit a prediction rule using one group of observations
  3. Compare its predictions with actual outcomes in a separate group
  4. Report how much better it performs than a simple benchmark

In a causal study, machine learning can help account for differences between groups before estimating an effect

  • A prediction rule might be a regression, a tree, or an average of many trees
  • The separate group provides evidence about performance on new observations from a similar setting
  • Prediction accuracy alone does not identify a causal effect

How does Facebook recognize faces in photos?

Algorithms use a large dataset of photos labeled as having a face or not to estimate a function f(x)f(x) that predicts the presence yy of a face from pixels xx.

This similarity to econometrics raises questions:

  • Are these algorithms merely applying standard techniques to novel and large datasets?
  • If there are fundamentally new empirical tools, how do they fit with what we know?
  • As empirical economists, how can we use them?

Source: Mullainathan and Spiess (2017), page 87.

  • Mullainathan and Spiess open with this example because the setup is familiar to economists: an outcome yy, inputs xx, and a function to estimate.
  • The difference is in the goal. A face detector is judged only on whether it labels new photos correctly; no one asks for the coefficient on a particular pixel.
  • Transition: the next slide states that difference formally.

Different goals: prediction and parameter estimation

Many economic applications estimate a parameter, such as a treatment effect or price sensitivity

Causal inference in a RF sense asks us to predict outcomes under conditions that were not observed, trying to replicate the gold standard of a RCT

Supervised machine learning methods are often designed to predict an outcome accurately for new observations

Note: Economists can also use prediction methods within procedures that estimate effects

This lecture covers where these methods came from, the three roles they play in empirical economics, and how a methods section reports them

Source: Mullainathan and Spiess (2017), pages 87 to 88.

  • The designs in 6.1 and the models in 6.2 recover parameters: a treatment effect, a price coefficient, an elasticity
  • Prediction is the direct goal in Role 1 and a component of the effect-estimation procedure in Role 3
  • Transition: these methods developed largely outside economics, which explains much about how economists use them.

Background

Machine learning’s use in applied economics grew over the past 20 years

Period Development
1984 to 2001 Regression trees (Breiman et al., 1984), the LASSO (Tibshirani, 1996), and random forests (Breiman, 2001)
1990s Neural networks studied in econometrics, but they “did not catch on at the time”
2001 Breiman’s “two cultures”: statistics relied on data models, while an algorithmic culture grew in other fields
2014 Early reviews for economists: Varian; Einav and Levin; Belloni, Chernozhukov, and Hansen
2017 to 2019 Mullainathan and Spiess; causal forests (Wager and Athey); Athey and Imbens’s review for economists
2020 to 2025 Reviews and special issues in agricultural and applied economics: Storm et al. (2020); Maruejols et al. (2025)

Sources: Athey and Imbens (2019), pages 686 to 688, 696 to 698, and 701; Mullainathan and Spiess (2017), page 87; Storm et al. (2020); Maruejols et al. (2025).

  • The methods themselves are older; their adoption in applied economics grew across the 2000s and 2010s.
  • Athey and Imbens open with Breiman (2001): one culture “assumes that the data are generated by a given stochastic data model”; the other “uses algorithmic models and treats the data mechanism as unknown.” Econometrics belongs to the first culture.
  • Statistics has since absorbed the algorithmic approach; Athey and Imbens note that “the adoption of these methods in economics has been slower.”
  • The dates for the original methods come from the citations in Athey and Imbens.
  • Transition: why adoption was slow, and why it accelerated.

Why economists adopted machine learning

  1. More readily available access to “big data”

    • Properties: many units, many variables per unit, and unstructured sources such as satellite images and text
    • Challenge organizing and studying this type of data (or even to decide which variables to construct) without a simplifying conceptual framework
    • Better data allow for (1) sharper tests of existing models, (2) tests of theories that had previously been difficult to assess, and (3) assistance choosing controls or functional forms

  2. Prediction questions

    • Some policy problems require an accurate forecast and no causal parameter

Sources: Athey and Imbens (2019), pages 686 to 687; Mullainathan and Spiess (2017), pages 88, 98, and 102, Einav and Levin (2014)

  • Einav and Levin describe the shift from small government surveys to administrative and private-sector data with near-universal coverage. They argue that economic models become more useful with such data, because they tell the researcher which variables to construct.
  • An example of a prediction question, from Mullainathan and Spiess: a judge deciding on bail must predict whether a defendant will return for trial. The decision requires a forecast, and no causal parameter (Kleinberg et al., as discussed on page 102).
  • Transition: why economics was slower to adopt these tools than statistics.

Why adoption was slow

Economics journals emphasize formal properties of estimators, such as consistency and asymptotic normality, which early machine learning methods did not provide

However, recent work established inference for adapted methods, including double selection and causal forests

Sources: Athey and Imbens (2019), pages 686 to 687; Mullainathan and Spiess (2017), pages 88, 98, and 102.

  • Athey and Imbens: machine learning research focuses on “working properties of algorithms in specific settings,” with formal results such as error rates, while econometrics focuses on large-sample properties of estimators and tests.
  • The methods economists now use are often adaptations: they modify machine learning algorithms to deliver valid confidence intervals for causal parameters. Two appear later in this lecture.
  • Transition: where machine learning sits among the AI-based methods economists now use.

How machine learning works

How a house-price prediction rule learns

Imagine we have recent home sales and want to predict the sale price of another house

  1. For each sold house, record its sale price and characteristics such as size and location
  2. Set aside some houses before fitting a model
  3. Use the remaining houses to fit a rule that turns characteristics into predicted prices
  4. Measure each mistake by comparing a prediction with the actual sale price

For a house that sold for $300,000, a prediction of $320,000 misses by $20,000

Is this rule sufficient, or should it be adjusted to reduce the average mistake on the houses used for fitting?

Source: Mullainathan and Spiess (2017), page 89

  • The observed price is yy, the house characteristics are xx, and the fitted prediction is f̂(x)\hat f(x)
  • A loss function gives a score to each mistake. Absolute error scores this example as 20,000 dollars. Squared error gives larger mistakes more weight
  • The observations used for fitting form the training sample

How do we score a house-price prediction?

Suppose a house sold for $300,000 and our model predicted $320,000.

Loss function Score for this house What it emphasizes
Absolute error |320−300|=20\lvert 320-300\rvert=20 The size of each mistake
Squared error (320−300)2=400(320-300)^2=400 Larger mistakes receive more weight

A loss function measures how wrong a prediction is for one observation.

For squared error:

L(f̂(xi),yi)=(yi−f̂(xi))2 L(\hat{f}(x_i),y_i)=(y_i-\hat{f}(x_i))^2

Here, yiy_i is the actual price and f̂(xi)\hat{f}(x_i) is the predicted price.

Source: Mullainathan and Spiess (2017), page 89

  • Prices are measured in thousands of dollars; squared errors have squared units.
  • The loss function assigns a penalty to each prediction error.
  • Squared error is used in ordinary least squares.
  • Different loss functions may favor different prediction rules.
  • Transition: One prediction error tells us very little about how well a model performs overall. We want a rule that makes small errors across many houses.

What are we trying to minimize?

We want a prediction rule that makes the smallest mistakes, on average, for new houses.

This is called expected loss:

E(Y,X)[L(f̂(X),Y)] E_{(Y,X)}[L(\hat{f}(X),Y)]

For squared error:

E(Y,X)[(Y−f̂(X))2] E_{(Y,X)}[(Y-\hat{f}(X))^2]

  • LL measures the loss for one house.
  • EE takes the average loss across the population.
  • f̂(X)\hat{f}(X) predicts price using house characteristics.

The goal: Find the prediction rule f̂\hat{f} that minimizes expected loss.

Source: Mullainathan and Spiess (2017), page 89

  • Expected loss extends the idea of a loss function from one observation to the population.
  • The expectation is taken over the joint distribution of house prices (Y) and characteristics (X).
  • We want a rule that predicts well for future houses drawn from the same population.
  • Expected loss is a population quantity. We cannot calculate it exactly because we do not observe the entire population.
  • Transition: If we cannot calculate expected loss directly, how do we determine whether a prediction rule is any good?

How do we evaluate a prediction rule?

We cannot directly calculate expected loss because we do not observe every possible future house.

Instead, we use the data we have:

  1. Training data: Choose a prediction rule that minimizes average loss across observed houses. This is called empirical loss.

    1n∑i=1nL(f̂(xi),yi) \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(\hat{f}(x_i),y_i)

Source: Mullainathan and Spiess (2017), pages 89 to 90

Testing the prediction rule

  1. Test data: Apply the fitted rule to houses that were set aside and not used to train the model.

  2. Evaluate: Calculate average loss on the test houses to estimate how well the rule predicts new observations.

Why? A rule that performs well on training data may not predict well on new data.

The next paper reports held-out R2R^2 as a summary of predictive fit. Higher held-out R2R^2 means better predictions relative to predicting the same average for every house

Source: Mullainathan and Spiess (2017), pages 89 to 90

  • Empirical loss is the average observed loss in the sample.
  • When squared-error loss is used, empirical loss is the mean squared error (MSE).
  • The algorithm uses training data to fit the prediction rule.
  • Evaluating the rule on the same data used to train it can produce an overly optimistic assessment of predictive performance.
  • Test observations provide an estimate of out-of-sample performance, assuming they represent the population of interest.
  • Set test observations aside before model selection and tuning.
  • The next paper uses held-out R-squared to summarize predictive performance.

An example: predicting house values with OLS and machine learning

Using data on 51,808 homes from the American Housing Survey:

  1. Fit OLS and several machine learning models using 10,000 homes and 150 characteristics
  2. Predict values for the remaining 41,808 homes
  3. Compare prediction accuracy using held-out R2R^2

Two findings:

  • Random forest: 45.5% held-out R2R^2, compared with 41.7% for OLS
  • Random forest: 85.1% training R2R^2, but only 45.5% held-out R2R^2

Source: Mullainathan and Spiess (2017), pages 89 to 90 and Table 1.

  • The outcome is log house value.
  • The first finding is that a flexible machine learning method can improve prediction accuracy relative to OLS.
  • The second finding is that performance on training data can substantially overstate performance on unseen observations.
  • The random forest’s training R-squared is 85.1%, compared with 45.5% for held-out observations.
  • The bracketed values in the table are 95% confidence intervals.
  • The improvement over OLS also differs across the distribution of house values.
  • Transition: What does a machine learning algorithm do differently from OLS to generate its predictions? Let’s start with a regression tree.

How does a regression tree predict house values?

Unlike OLS, a regression tree does not require us to specify a linear relationship. Instead, it divides houses into groups with similar values.

  1. Start with all houses in one group
  2. Find the yes-or-no split that produces the greatest reduction in squared prediction error (here, house type)
  3. Repeat within each new group, using characteristics such as bathrooms, rooms, and square footage
  4. Predict the value of a house using the average value of houses in its final group (a leaf)

Source: Mullainathan and Spiess (2017), pages 90 to 91 and Figure 1.

  • Table 1 showed that machine learning methods can outperform OLS on held-out observations. We now examine how these methods construct prediction rules, beginning with regression trees.
  • OLS estimates coefficients for a specified set of variables and interactions. A regression tree searches for splits that group houses with similar outcomes.
  • Each split is selected to reduce squared prediction error in the training data.
  • Read the leftmost path: a house of type 2, 3, or 7, with fewer than 1.5 bathrooms and fewer than 4.5 rooms, is predicted to have a log value of 9.2.
  • The prediction at each leaf is the average log house value among the training observations in that group.
  • A regression tree effectively selects interactions among characteristics without requiring the researcher to specify them in advance.
  • Random forests build on this idea by combining predictions from many trees.
  • Transition: If each additional split can improve the fit to training data, why not keep dividing houses into smaller groups?

Why not keep dividing the tree?

Each additional split allows a regression tree to fit the training houses more closely.

  1. More splits create smaller groups of similar houses
  2. Smaller groups can reduce prediction errors in the training data
  3. But very specific groups may capture patterns unique to those houses
  4. Those patterns may not hold for new observations

Overfitting occurs when a model fits the training data closely but predicts poorly on new data.

Regularization limits model flexibility to improve out-of-sample prediction.

Source: Mullainathan and Spiess (2017), pages 91 to 93 and Table 2.

  • Each split is selected to reduce training error, but reductions in training error do not necessarily improve predictions on new observations.
  • A sufficiently deep tree can nearly memorize the training observations.
  • This helps explain why the random forest’s training R-squared is so much higher than its held-out R-squared, although the gap alone does not establish which tree settings are optimal.
  • Regularization constrains model complexity.
  • For regression trees, we can limit tree depth, the number of splits, or the minimum number of observations in a leaf.
  • Restricting flexibility may increase training error while improving held-out performance.
  • Transition: How do we determine the appropriate amount of flexibility?

How do we choose the right tree depth?

A deeper tree may fit the training data better, but how deep should it be?

Cross-validation evaluates different model settings using only the training data.

  1. Divide the training data into several groups (folds)
  2. Fit the model using all but one fold
  3. Predict outcomes for the excluded fold and calculate prediction error
  4. Repeat until every fold has been excluded once
  5. Choose the tree depth with the lowest average prediction error

Then fit the selected model on all training data and evaluate it on the held-out test data.

Sources: Mullainathan and Spiess (2017), pages 92 to 93; Bittmann (2025), Section 3.

  • Cross-validation creates repeated prediction exercises within the training sample.
  • For example, in five-fold cross-validation, the model is trained five times, each time leaving out a different fifth of the training observations.
  • Repeat the process for different tree depths and compare their average validation errors.
  • After selecting the depth, refit the model using the entire training sample.
  • The final test sample is reserved for evaluating performance after model selection.
  • Cross-validation helps select model settings, but it does not guarantee that the selected model will perform well in a different population.
  • With panel or spatial data, cross-validation should respect the structure of the observations.
  • Transition: The same principle applies to other methods. What differs is how each method constructs and constrains its prediction rule.

How do the methods differ?

Each method learns a prediction rule from training data, but differs in how it generates predictions and controls complexity.

Method How it predicts How it controls flexibility
OLS Estimates a linear combination of characteristics Researcher specifies variables and interactions
Regression tree Divides observations into groups Limits on tree depth and group size

Sources: Mullainathan and Spiess (2017), pages 92 to 94 and Table 2; Athey and Imbens (2019), pages 690 and 695 to 699.

How do the methods differ? (continued)

Method How it predicts How it controls flexibility
LASSO Estimates a linear combination of characteristics Penalizes coefficients, shrinking some to zero
Random forest Averages predictions from many trees Randomization, averaging, and tree settings

Common goal: Minimize prediction error on new, unseen observations.

Sources: Mullainathan and Spiess (2017), pages 92 to 94 and Table 2; Athey and Imbens (2019), pages 690 and 695 to 699.

  • The methods differ in the class of prediction rules they consider and how they limit complexity.
  • OLS specifies the functional form in advance. Its flexibility depends on the variables and interactions included.
  • Regression trees discover splits and interactions from the data.
  • LASSO adds a penalty to the least-squares objective. Larger penalties shrink coefficients toward zero, and some become exactly zero.
  • Random forests fit many trees using randomized observations and predictors, then average their predictions.
  • Cross-validation can help select the amount of LASSO penalization, tree depth, and other model settings.
  • All methods can be evaluated using the same held-out prediction metrics, such as MSE or R-squared.
  • Transition: We have seen how machine learning methods improve prediction. Next, we consider how they can be used in economic research.

Examples of how machine learning is used in empirical economics

1. Prediction as the objective: Predicts an outcome, such as household food insecurity

2. Prediction within a larger analysis: Selects the variables that enter an economic model or a treatment-effect regression

3. Heterogeneous treatment effects: Estimates how a treatment effect varies across farms or households

Sources: Mullainathan and Spiess (2017); Athey and Imbens (2019), pages 686 to 688; Maruejols et al. (2025).

  • Each role has its own section later in the lecture, with one published paper.
  • In roles 2 and 3, the machine learning step sits inside a causal or structural analysis, so the identification questions from 6.1 still apply.
  • Transition: first, an application where predicting the outcome is the objective

Role 1: Prediction as the objective

Zhao et al. (2025): predicting calorie poverty

Research question: Can household and market characteristics help identify households at risk of inadequate calorie intake?

  1. Use three days of food records to measure calorie intake for 1,080 rural households
  2. Classify each household as calorie poor or not calorie poor
  3. Use household, village, and food-market characteristics to predict that classification
  4. Fit a random forest, LASSO, and logit model using 756 households
  5. Evaluate predictions on the remaining 324 households

Unlike house values, the outcome is binary: a household is either classified as calorie poor or not.

Source: Zhao et al. (2025), Sections 3 to 5.

  • This is supervised learning because the outcome is observed and used to train the prediction models.
  • The outcome is binary rather than continuous, so the authors evaluate classification accuracy rather than regression R-squared.
  • The calorie-poverty threshold determines which households receive the label.
  • The households are in Jilin, Shandong, and Hubei. Approximately 61.1% of the full sample is classified as calorie poor.
  • The authors use SMOTE to address class imbalance and out-of-bag observations to help tune the forest.
  • Transition: Does the random forest classify households more accurately than conventional approaches?

Does machine learning improve classification?

Model Test-sample accuracy
Random forest 65.7%
LASSO 61.1%
Logit benchmark 58.9%

Accuracy measures the percentage of households classified correctly.

The random forest improves accuracy by 6.8 percentage points relative to logit.

But accuracy does not tell us which households were misclassified.

  • False positive: A household is predicted to be calorie poor when it is not
  • False negative: A calorie-poor household is not identified

Source: Zhao et al. (2025), Sections 3 to 5 and Table 3.

  • The methods are evaluated on the same held-out observations.
  • A model’s accuracy depends on the classification threshold.
  • For a food assistance program, false negatives may be especially consequential because eligible households could be overlooked.
  • The paper also reports sensitivity, specificity, and area under the ROC curve.
  • The full-sample calorie-poverty prevalence is 61.1%; that is not necessarily the accuracy of a majority-class classifier in the test sample.
  • Transition: The authors also examine which variables are most useful for prediction. What can we learn from those rankings?

Do important predictors identify causes?

Zhao et al. find that household income and access to food markets help predict calorie poverty.

But a variable that improves prediction is not necessarily a cause of the outcome.

  • A predictor may be correlated with other factors that affect calorie poverty
  • Highly correlated predictors may provide similar information
  • LASSO may select one predictor and exclude another, depending on the sample

Predictor importance tells us what helps classify households, not what would happen if we changed that characteristic.

Sources: Zhao et al. (2025), Section 5.2; Mullainathan and Spiess (2017), page 97, Figure 2; Stetter et al. (2022), page 740, footnote 17.

  • The distinction is between predicting an outcome and identifying a causal effect.
  • Income may predict calorie poverty, but this analysis does not identify the effect of increasing income.
  • Mullainathan and Spiess demonstrate that LASSO may select different variables across different partitions of the same data.
  • This is especially relevant when predictors are highly correlated.
  • Prediction can help identify households for assistance without establishing why those households experience inadequate calorie intake.
  • Transition: In the next examples, prediction is not the final objective. Instead, machine learning helps researchers estimate economic relationships.

Role 2: Prediction within a larger analysis

Li and Yu (2025): selecting apple attributes

Research question: How much are consumers willing to pay for apple attributes such as origin, size, and production method?

  1. Ask 100 consumers to choose between apples with different attributes and a no-purchase option
  2. Use LASSO to identify which attributes help predict consumer choices
  3. Estimate a conditional logit model using the selected attributes
  4. Calculate willingness to pay from the estimated coefficients

Role of ML: Select variables to include in an economic model.

Source: Li and Yu (2025), Sections 2 and 3.

  • Each respondent completes four choice scenarios.
  • The authors use LASSO to select apple attributes, while always retaining price.
  • Tenfold cross-validation determines the LASSO penalty.
  • The final conditional logit estimates relationships between attributes and choice probabilities.
  • Willingness to pay is calculated as the negative ratio of the attribute and price coefficients.
  • The prediction step helps specify the economic model rather than serving as the final result.
  • Transition: Which attributes does LASSO retain?

How does LASSO select attributes?

LASSO estimates coefficients while penalizing their absolute magnitude.

As the penalty increases, some coefficients shrink to zero.

In Li and Yu (2025):

  • Tenfold cross-validation determines the penalty
  • Organic and Japan have coefficients of zero at the selected penalty
  • Price remains because the authors exclude it from the penalty

The excluded attributes provide little additional predictive information in this fitted model, given the other attributes.

Does a coefficient of zero mean consumers do not value the attribute?

Source: Li and Yu (2025), Section 3.1, Figure 2, and Table 4.

  • LASSO minimizes prediction error plus a penalty on coefficient magnitude.
  • A larger penalty generally produces a simpler model.
  • Cross-validation selects the penalty based on predictive performance.
  • A zero coefficient is a model-selection result, not evidence that every consumer ignores the attribute.
  • Correlated attributes may substitute for one another.
  • The authors also use Shapley values to evaluate attribute importance.
  • Transition: How does this selection affect the economic estimates?

LASSO changes willingness-to-pay estimates

Estimated willingness to pay relative to China, in euros per kg:

Apple attribute All attributes After LASSO selection
France 1.20 1.53
Italy 1.42 1.40
Small apples 1.30 1.41
Organic −0.04 Excluded

Source: Li and Yu (2025), Section 3, Tables 6 and 7.

What changes when LASSO selects attributes?

After removing the selected attributes, estimated willingness to pay changes for some of the remaining characteristics.

LASSO selects variables based on their contribution to the model’s predictive objective, subject to its penalty.

But the variables that produce the best predictions are not necessarily the variables we need to estimate a particular economic parameter.

Source: Li and Yu (2025), Section 3, Tables 6 and 7.

  • The comparison is between a conditional logit model containing all attributes and a model excluding the attributes selected for removal by LASSO.
  • Willingness to pay for French apples increases from €1.20 to €1.53 per kilogram.
  • The estimate for Italian apples changes little.
  • Organic willingness to pay is no longer estimated in the selected specification.
  • Selecting variables for predictive performance does not automatically produce unbiased estimates or valid inference for every economic parameter.
  • Discussion: If organic production is a primary research interest, should the researcher allow LASSO to exclude it?
  • The variables included in a model affect its estimated coefficients and willingness-to-pay estimates. Selecting variables for prediction does not necessarily produce the best model for answering an economic research question.
  • Transition: What if we use machine learning to select control variables when estimating a treatment effect?

Role 3: Heterogeneous treatment effects

How does prediction help estimate an effect?

Suppose an agri-environment program encourages farmers to reduce fertiliser use.

The research question is: Do programs have the same effect on every farm?

The average treatment effect tells us how participation changes fertiliser use across farms.

But the effect may differ depending on:

  • Farm size
  • Location
  • Crop production and yield potential
  • Other farm characteristics

Sources: Athey and Imbens (2019), pages 701 to 704; Stetter et al. (2022), Sections 2.3 and 4.1 to 4.3.

Conditional average treatment effects

Conditional average treatment effects (CATEs) describe how estimated treatment effects differ across farms with different characteristics.

τ(x)=E[Y(1)−Y(0)∣X=x] \tau(x)=E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid X=x]

Sources: Athey and Imbens (2019), pages 701 to 704; Stetter et al. (2022), Sections 2.3 and 4.1 to 4.3.

  • This extends the potential-outcomes framework introduced earlier in the course.
  • Y(1) is the potential outcome under program participation; Y(0) is the potential outcome without participation.
  • The average treatment effect summarizes the mean difference across the population.
  • The conditional average treatment effect describes how that difference varies with observed characteristics.
  • The objective is not simply to predict fertiliser use, but to estimate differences in treatment effects.
  • Transition: How can machine learning help estimate these differences?

How does a causal forest differ from a regression tree?

A regression tree divides observations into groups to predict an outcome.

A causal forest seeks groups with different treatment effects.

  1. Predict treatment participation using observed characteristics
  2. Predict the outcome using observed characteristics
  3. Account for those predicted relationships when comparing treated and untreated farms
  4. Use many trees to estimate how treatment effects vary across farm characteristics

The goal: Estimate treatment-effect heterogeneity rather than minimize ordinary outcome prediction error.

Sources: Athey and Imbens (2019), pages 701 to 704; Stetter et al. (2022), Sections 2.3 and 4.1 to 4.3.

  • Standard prediction forests estimate relationships between predictors and observed outcomes.
  • Causal forests adapt tree-based methods to estimate variation in treatment effects.
  • In Stetter et al., machine learning estimates treatment participation and expected outcomes as intermediate steps.
  • The causal forest uses adjusted comparisons to estimate heterogeneous effects.
  • The method does not automatically eliminate selection bias.
  • The credibility of the estimates depends on the identification assumptions.
  • Transition: Let’s look at an application to agri-environment programs.

Stetter et al. (2022): do program effects differ across farms?

Research question: Does participation in an agri-environment scheme change farm outcomes, and do those effects vary across farms?

  1. Observe 2,758 Bavarian farms in 2014, including 1,641 program participants
  2. Collect farm characteristics, program participation, and environmental outcomes
  3. Use machine learning to account for observed differences between participating and nonparticipating farms
  4. Estimate heterogeneous treatment effects using a causal forest

The authors examine four outcomes, including fertiliser expenditure, pesticide expenditure, land-use diversity, and emissions.

Source: Stetter et al. (2022), Sections 2.3, 4.1, and 4.2.

  • The study uses more than 130 farm and county characteristics.
  • Participation and outcome models are estimated using random forests.
  • The causal forest estimates conditional average treatment effects.
  • The aim is to identify whether effects differ by observable farm characteristics.
  • Settings are tuned using cross-validation.
  • The authors use large forests to obtain stable estimates.
  • Transition: What do the authors find?

Which farms have detectable program effects?

Outcome Share of farms with a statistically detectable effect
Fertiliser expenditure 33% show reductions
Land-use diversity 55% show increases
Pesticide expenditure 7% show improvements
Emissions Under 1% show reductions; about 4% show increases

Among farms with detectable reductions in fertiliser expenditure, the estimated reduction averages €14 per hectare. Estimated effects vary by location, farm size, and yield potential. Policy relevance: Differences in estimated effects may inform how program payments are targeted.

Source: Stetter et al. (2022), Sections 5.2, 5.3, and 6 and Table 3.

  • A statistically detectable effect means the reported confidence interval excludes zero.
  • The percentages describe statistical detectability, which depends on both estimated effect magnitude and precision.
  • An insignificant estimate does not mean the true treatment effect is zero.
  • The effects vary across farm characteristics, suggesting that average effects can hide meaningful heterogeneity.
  • The authors examine how these differences might inform program targeting.
  • These are estimates from an observational study, so the identifying assumptions remain important.
  • Transition: How do the authors justify interpreting these differences as treatment effects?

What makes a causal forest estimate credible?

A causal forest can estimate differences in treatment effects, but machine learning alone does not establish causality.

The analysis relies on two important assumptions:

  1. Selection on observables: After accounting for recorded characteristics, treated and untreated farms are comparable in their potential outcomes
  2. Overlap: Farms with similar characteristics have a positive probability of participating or not participating

Stetter et al. exclude 23 farms that lack suitable comparisons and examine sensitivity to unobserved confounding.

The distinction: Machine learning helps estimate relationships; causal identification determines whether they can be interpreted as treatment effects.

Source: Stetter et al. (2022), Sections 4.3 and 5.1.

  • Selection on observables assumes that the recorded characteristics account for the confounding relevant to treatment assignment.
  • Overlap requires comparable treated and untreated farms across the covariate region used for estimation.
  • The retained farms have estimated participation probabilities between 0.27 and 0.86.
  • The authors use placebo tests and simulations involving omitted confounders.
  • Sensitivity analysis can investigate robustness but cannot establish that all relevant confounders were observed.
  • Machine learning provides flexible estimation, not an identification strategy by itself.
  • Transition: What should we look for when assessing machine learning in an empirical economics paper?

What role does machine learning play in economic research?

Role Research objective Contribution of ML
Prediction Identify households at risk of calorie poverty Improve classification accuracy
Model selection and estimation Estimate preferences or a coefficient of interest Select variables and controls
Heterogeneous treatment effects Estimate how program impacts differ across farms Flexibly estimate differences in treatment effects

Sources: Mullainathan and Spiess (2017); Athey and Imbens (2019); Zhao et al. (2025); Li and Yu (2025); Belloni et al. (2014); Stetter et al. (2022).

Three questions to ask when reading a paper

  1. What is the quantity the researchers want to estimate?
  2. What does machine learning contribute to that objective?
  3. What assumptions are needed to interpret the results?

Sources: Mullainathan and Spiess (2017); Athey and Imbens (2019); Zhao et al. (2025); Li and Yu (2025); Belloni et al. (2014); Stetter et al. (2022).

  • Prediction accuracy is the primary objective in the first example.
  • In the second role, ML is used to support an economic model or the estimation of a particular parameter.
  • In the third role, ML helps estimate treatment-effect heterogeneity.
  • Better prediction does not necessarily imply better parameter estimates or more credible causal inference.
  • The research objective determines how the model should be evaluated.

Reporting a machine learning method

What to report in a machine learning methods section

Item Example
1. What the method does in the study Zhao et al. use it to predict calorie poverty
2. What is predicted and which information is used Calorie poverty from household and market characteristics
3. Which observations are used for fitting and testing 70% training, 30% test
4. How method settings are chosen Cross-validation; number of trees
5. How well it performs on test observations Classification accuracy, compared with logit
6. If effects are claimed, what supports the causal comparison Stetter et al. discuss comparable farms and overlap
7. How another researcher could repeat the analysis Software, package versions, random seed, and code

Sources: Zhao et al. (2025); Li and Yu (2025); Stetter et al. (2022); Athey and Imbens (2019), pages 686 to 687.

  • Items 1 to 5 describe the prediction step. Item 6 is essential when the paper interprets a result as an effect
  • Athey and Imbens: researchers “should articulate clearly the goals of their analysis and why certain properties of algorithms and estimators may or may not be important.” Item 1 does that.
  • Item 7 matters for replication, as in Unit 5: results depend on random sample splits, so report the software, versions, and random seed.
  • Transition: questions for the proposal.

What to consider for your proposal

  • The role: does the question concern predicting an outcome, selecting variables, or how an effect varies?
  • The claim: does the conclusion require a causal effect? If so, what is the design, and where does machine learning enter it?
  • The benchmark: which simpler model or rule should the method outperform?
  • The evidence: which out-of-sample measure, or which identification check, will the paper report?
  • Most proposals in this course will not use machine learning, but every student should be able to locate items 1 to 7 in a paper that does.
  • Students who use these methods should state the role in the first sentence of the methods section.
