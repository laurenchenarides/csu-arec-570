What should a framework accomplish?

  • Explain why the research question has an economic mechanism.
  • Identify the people making choices and the constraints they face.
  • Derive a prediction that could be contradicted by evidence.
  • Connect the model to the variables we can observe.

Adapted from Unit 4, slides 3–6. Ask students to describe their mechanism before naming an estimator.

A model is a deliberate simplification

A useful model leaves things out. The question is whether it leaves out something essential to the argument.

  • Which details are necessary for the mechanism?
  • Which assumptions make the analysis manageable?
  • Which conclusions would change if an assumption failed?

A regression coefficient alone does not explain a causal mechanism.

From a problem to a model

Following Ethridge:

  1. Locate the problem in the literature.
  2. Strip it down to the relevant choices and constraints.
  3. Start with a simple theoretical structure.
  4. Add the features needed for this question.
  5. Derive hypotheses and identify the evidence needed to assess them.

Source: Ethridge (2004).

Ask Varian’s questions

  • Who makes the choices?
  • What do these agents want?
  • How do they interact?
  • What constraints do they face?
  • What adjusts when their choices are inconsistent?

Discussion: Which answer is least clear in your proposed project?

Source: Varian (1997), assigned pp. 4–6.

Example: irrigation under a water price

Illustrative classroom model: A farmer chooses water use ww.

maxw≥0pf(w)−cw \max_{w\geq 0}\; p f(w)-cw

  • p>0p>0: output price.
  • c>0c>0: price per unit of water.
  • f(w)f(w): production, with f′(w)>0f'(w)>0 and f″(w)<0f''(w)<0.

For an interior optimum, pf′(w*)=cpf'(w^*)=c.

Derive the prediction

Differentiating the first-order condition gives

pf″(w*)dw*dc=1⇒dw*dc=1pf″(w*)<0. p f''(w^*)\frac{dw^*}{dc}=1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{dw^*}{dc}=\frac{1}{p f''(w^*)}<0.

Conditional prediction: Higher water prices reduce optimal water use.

Which assumptions make the sign follow? What changes if water use is fixed by a binding quota?

New classroom example. Hold output price, technology, and other inputs fixed. The derivative assumes a differentiable, strictly concave production function and an interior solution.

Discussion: Ethridge and Varian

  • How does simplifying a problem help us understand it?
  • At what point does simplification remove the mechanism we care about?
  • Would the irrigation model answer a question about farm income, water use, or both?
  • What additional structure would an income question require?

Bring one assumption from your own project into the discussion.

Cheng: discovery and exposition

Finding an argument and presenting it are different tasks.

A readable proof states:

  1. Definitions and assumptions.
  2. A sequence of justified steps.
  3. The conclusion that those steps establish.

Source: Cheng (2004).

Discussion: find the missing step

A student writes:

Water becomes more expensive, so farmers adopt efficient irrigation, and total water use falls.

  • Which part describes a choice? Which part is a prediction?
  • Could adoption change irrigated acreage or crop choice?
  • What assumptions are needed to conclude that total water use falls?
  • Rewrite the argument so each conclusion follows from a stated premise.

New exercise applying Cheng’s guidance. This is a hypothetical claim to diagnose, not a claim about observed irrigation outcomes.

What Romer calls mathiness

Romer challenges arguments in which the connection between formal symbols, verbal claims, and economic meaning becomes unclear.

  • Do the terms mean the same thing throughout the argument?
  • Does the mathematical result support the verbal conclusion?
  • Can the assumptions and derivation be checked?

Source: Romer (2015).

Discussion: evaluate Romer’s argument

  • What specific example does Romer use to support his criticism?
  • How does he distinguish mathematical theory from mathiness?
  • Which claims concern the models, and which concern incentives in the profession?
  • What evidence would make you revise your assessment of his argument?

Support your answer with a passage from the reading.

The source deck frames several contested interpretations as conclusions. This discussion asks students to assess those interpretations against the paper.

A check against our own biases

  • Am I choosing assumptions because they clarify the problem or deliver a preferred result?
  • Have I separated a model implication from an empirical claim?
  • Would I accept the same argument if its conclusion were inconvenient?
  • What criticism would improve the framework?

Listen for the implicit model

At a seminar, identify:

  • The agents and their incentives.
  • The mechanism linking the explanatory variable to the outcome.
  • The assumptions connecting the evidence to that mechanism.

Then ask a question about a specific missing link.

Workshop: your framework

Write a short account of your project:

  1. Research question.
  2. Agents, choices, and constraints.
  3. Mechanism.
  4. Testable prediction.
  5. One assumption that could fail.

Exchange with a partner. Mark the step that needs the most explanation.

Before you leave

Complete both sentences:

  • “My model predicts ___ because ___.”
  • “That prediction depends on ___.”
