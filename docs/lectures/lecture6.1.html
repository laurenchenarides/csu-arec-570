What must the empirical strategy section accomplish?

But first, where the empirical strategy section lives in your paper

  1. Introduction
  2. Background or Literature Review
  3. Conceptual Framework
  4. Data and Summary Evidence
  5. Empirical Strategy & Identification Strategy
  6. Results
  7. Discussion (or Policy Implications, or Managerial Implications)
  8. Conclusion

Bellemare calls this section the Empirical Framework, and some papers place it before the data section (Bellemare 2020).

Bellemare’s outline for an applied paper: Introduction, Theoretical Framework, Data and Descriptive Statistics, Empirical Framework, Results and Discussion, Summary and Concluding Remarks. He notes that the data and empirical sections are often swapped; the Dalheimer and Bellemare paper on the next slides puts the empirical framework first.

Two differences from his outline. He argues that a separate literature review is “almost always entirely unwarranted” in a journal article and recommends a short review of the five to ten closest studies in the introduction; a separate review fits a thesis or dissertation chapter. He recommends a background section when the reader needs institutional detail, such as the provisions of a law.

The empirical strategy section turns a prediction from your framework into an estimating equation

  • Conceptual framework (Unit 4) gives you guidance on developing testable predictions
  • Data (Unit 5) gives you guidance on describing your data, the unit of observation, and the measures that make your data fit for use
  • Methods (this unit) explains how to turn the conceptual model into an equation with estimable parameters, and why the data identify them
  • A referee should be able to judge the design before seeing a result

This slide places the section in the paper. The rest of the lecture follows the order of the section itself: the estimation strategy (equation, estimator, inference), then the identification strategy (assumption, threats, robustness), then one research question answered three ways.

Four terms come up in every empirical strategy section

  • Parameter of interest: the coefficient that answers your research question
  • Estimator: the rule that turns data into an estimate of that parameter, such as OLS, maximum likelihood, or a difference in means
  • Identifying assumption: the condition under which the parameter you estimate equals the relationship you want to learn about
  • Inference: how you measure the uncertainty in the estimate, through standard errors, clustering, and hypothesis tests

Students often use “estimator” and “identification” interchangeably. They are separate choices. The same identifying assumption can be paired with different estimators, and a correct estimator cannot fix a comparison that is not identified.

Ask: which of the four is hardest to write down for your proposal right now?

Bellemare splits the section into what you estimate and why it identifies a causal effect

Estimation strategy

The equations, the estimator, and how inference is done

Identification strategy

The feature of the data that supports a causal interpretation, and what threatens it

Use these as the two parts of your section (Bellemare 2020).

This is the structure from the Bellemare reading in Lecture 2.3, Section 5. His Section 5.1 covers the equation, the estimator, hypothesis tests, and standard errors. Section 5.2 covers identification and the threats to it.

A published paper states the equation, then defines every term

Point out the sequence: the relationship in words, the equation, a definition of every term, then one sentence naming the parameter of interest. The next paragraph explains why they first-difference the equation, which is the estimator choice.

Two small inconsistencies are worth noticing: the text defines pitp_{it} for “county ii” in a country-level paper, and the equation uses e1ite_{1it} while the text defines eite_{it}. Referees catch these, and they are why every term should be defined with the same notation used in the equation.

Footnote 5 in the paper explains why the authors do not cluster standard errors after first-differencing. That is an inference decision stated with a reason.

Source: Dalheimer and Bellemare (2026), Section 2.1 (accepted version posted on Bellemare’s website).

The identification strategy explains what in the data supports a causal interpretation

The section walks through Bellemare’s threats in order: reverse causation, omitted variables, and measurement error. Only after naming them does it explain the shift-share instrumental variable design that addresses them. We return to this structure in the identification strategy section.

Source: Dalheimer and Bellemare (2026), Section 2.2 (accepted version posted on Bellemare’s website).

Six questions organize the section, in the order they appear in a paper

Subsection Question Where the answer appears
Estimation strategy 1. What is the relationship of interest? The estimating equation and the parameter of interest
2. How will you measure uncertainty? The estimator, the standard errors, the clustering level, and why
Identification strategy 3. Is that relationship causal? Whether the parameter is causal, and if not, what it describes
4. What experiment would ideally measure it? The effect you are trying to estimate
5. What variation identifies it, and what must be true of that variation? The identifying assumption
6. What could make the identifying assumption fail? The threats, and the design choice or robustness check that handles each

Adapted from Angrist and Pischke (2009), Chapter 1, and Bellemare (2020), Section 5.

Angrist and Pischke ask four questions, starting with “What is the causal relationship of interest?” Not every paper estimates a causal relationship. A descriptive paper or a forecasting paper still needs an estimating equation; it states what the parameter describes and does not claim more. That is why the causal question is separate here.

The order follows Bellemare: the estimation strategy (equation, estimator, inference) comes first, then the identification strategy (assumption, threats, robustness). The rest of the lecture follows the same order.

What’s included in the estimation strategy subsection

Write the equation so a referee never has to ask what a symbol means

  • Latin letters for variables, Greek letters for parameters
  • Subscripts run from the smallest unit to the largest
  • Define every term directly below the equation
  • Give each specification its own coefficient label
  • The unit of observation matches the key in your data section

Source: Bellemare (2020), Section 5.1.

Bellemare also asks for the estimator by name (OLS, linear probability model, probit, maximum likelihood) and for the standard errors to say what they are robust to. “Robust standard errors” is not enough; “robust to heteroskedasticity” is.

The next slide explains the last rule. Lecture 6.2 opens with the first two.

The unit of observation describes a row; the key identifies it in the file

Unit of observation Key
What it is What one row represents, in words The field or fields whose values are unique for every row
Panel weather model A county in a year County FIPS code and year
Price-gouging data (Lecture 5.5) One product at one store in one week Store, product, and week identifiers
Where it appears The data section and the equation’s subscripts The code that builds and merges the data

The unit tells the reader what each row means. The key lets you check that there is exactly one row per unit before you estimate (Gentzkow and Shapiro 2014).

The two are the same idea at different levels: the unit is the description for the reader, and the key is how the computer enforces it. If two rows share the same key, either the key is incomplete or the data contain duplicates, and the estimate will weight some units twice. Gentzkow and Shapiro’s chapter on keys (Lecture 5.4) covers how to check this.

The estimation strategy also names the estimator and says how inference is done

  • The estimator by name, such as OLS, a linear probability model, probit, or maximum likelihood, and why
  • The hypothesis being tested
  • What the standard errors are robust to, such as heteroskedasticity; “robust standard errors” alone is not enough
  • Whether and how standard errors are clustered, at what level, and why
  • Whether sampling weights are used, and how they were constructed

Source: Bellemare (2020), Section 5.1.

The clustering level usually follows the level at which the treatment varies or at which errors are correlated. In the panel weather example later today, neighboring counties share weather shocks.

We return to inference later in the unit, including how to handle a small number of clusters.

What’s included in the identification strategy subsection

Identification asks whether your data and assumptions determine the parameter you want to estimate

  • Suppose you had data on the entire population. The parameter is identified if only one value of it is consistent with those data
  • If more than one value fits, the parameter is not identified, and a larger sample or a different estimator will not fix it
  • Whether a parameter is identified depends on assumptions about how the data were generated
  • A directed acyclic graph (DAG) draws those assumptions: each arrow is a causal effect, and each missing arrow is an assumption
  • Example: a paper wants to estimate the effect of military service on later earnings (Jurajda 2007, Example 1.1)

Bellemare (Section 5.2) uses “identification” in the sense most applied papers now use: causal identification, when a coefficient reflects a causal relationship rather than a partial correlation. He refers to Pearl’s notation for this, the effect of setting a variable rather than observing it.

The next two slides use this question to show a parameter that is not identified and two ways to identify it.

When an unobserved factor drives both treatment and outcome, the effect is not identified

A comparison of veterans and nonveterans picks up both the effect and the path through earnings potential. With unlimited data it still cannot separate them (Jurajda 2007, Example 1.1).

Jurajda asks: “Are the (minority) vets earning more because of the military service or are the high-earners more likely to enroll in the army?” The dashed path through earnings potential is selection bias, the term defined two slides from now.

The four threats map onto this picture. Unobserved heterogeneity is the dashed node. Reverse causality would be an arrow from earnings back to service. Measurement error means the service box is recorded with error. Spillovers would be an arrow from one person’s service to another person’s earnings.

The effect is identified if you measure the common causes or use variation they do not drive

Panel A is selection on observed characteristics. The military screens applicants on age, schooling, and test scores, so comparing veterans and nonveterans with the same values identifies the effect, if nothing else drives both. Angrist and Pischke note that this is “clearly not guaranteed”: applicants who drop out at the last minute may differ in earnings potential.

Panel B is an instrument. The lottery number changes who serves but is assigned at random, so earnings potential cannot affect it, and it should affect earnings only through service. It identifies the effect for men whose service depended on their lottery number.

The identifying assumption in each panel is the “Requires” line. That is the sentence a paper states in words.

A: Angrist (1998), discussed in Angrist and Pischke (2009), Chapter 3. B: the Vietnam-era draft lottery, Angrist (1990), in Jurajda (2007), Example 3.11.

The identifying assumption says why the groups you compare are comparable

  • Comparing treated and untreated units measures the treatment effect plus any difference that existed between the groups before treatment
  • Jurajda’s example: veterans may earn more because of military service, or because people with higher earnings potential were more likely to enlist
  • That pre-existing difference is selection bias
  • The identifying assumption states why selection bias is zero for your comparison
  • Write it in one sentence, without notation

Angrist and Pischke, Chapter 2, write this as a decomposition: the observed difference in means equals the average effect on the treated plus selection bias, the difference in untreated outcomes between the two groups. Students do not need the decomposition in their paper. They need the sentence that explains why the untreated outcomes of the two groups would have been the same.

Jurajda’s notes describe identification as a search for variation that is unrelated to these pre-existing differences.

Bellemare asks every paper to address four threats to identification

Threat What it means Common responses
Reverse causality The outcome affects the treatment Measure treatment before the outcome; use an instrument
Unobserved heterogeneity An omitted factor affects both treatment and outcome Fixed effects, controls, or a design that holds the factor constant
Measurement error The treatment is recorded with error; classical error biases the estimate toward zero A second measure; an instrument
Spillovers One unit’s treatment affects another unit’s outcome, now or later Define units so spillovers stay within them; test for them

Source: Bellemare (2020), Section 5.2.

Bellemare asks authors to discuss each threat in turn, say whether it applies, and explain how the paper handles it. The response column lists common approaches; it is not from Bellemare.

He adds: “If your results are not causally identified, do not lie about what they can and cannot do.”

The DAG from earlier shows each threat as a feature of the graph. The climate example later applies this table to one design.

A threat to identification biases the estimate; a problem with inference misstates its precision

  • Threat to identification: the estimate is centered on the wrong value, and a larger sample does not fix it
  • Problem with inference: the estimate may be centered correctly, but the standard errors are too small, so confidence intervals are too narrow and tests reject too often
  • Panel weather example: neighboring counties share weather shocks, so treating their errors as independent overstates precision; cluster at a level that contains that correlation and say why
  • Either problem leads a referee to doubt the result, so the section addresses both

Source: Bellemare (2020), Sections 5.1 and 5.2.

Students often list clustering as a threat. It is not a threat to identification: clustering does not change the point estimate. It changes how confident we are in it.

Robustness checks can address either problem: alternative samples or controls test the identifying assumption; alternative clustering levels test the inference choice.

We return to inference later in the unit, including how to handle a small number of clusters.

Robustness checks vary your choices; sensitivity analyses relax your assumptions

  • Robustness check: re-estimate with another defensible outcome measure, treatment measure, estimator, functional form, or sample, and show the conclusion holds (Bellemare 2020, sec. 6.2)
  • Sensitivity analysis: ask how far the identifying assumption would have to fail before the conclusion changes, for example how strong an omitted variable would have to be (Athey and Imbens 2017)
  • Placebo test: estimate an effect that should be zero; Bellemare counts these as robustness checks, and Athey and Imbens list them separately
  • Why papers include them: researchers try many specifications before choosing one, so readers need evidence that the result was not selected for (Athey and Imbens 2017)

A robustness check asks whether your choices matter. A sensitivity analysis asks how wrong your assumption could be.

Athey and Imbens call these “supplementary analyses”: their purpose is not a better estimate of the main effect but evidence on the credibility of the primary analysis. They cite Leamer on the gap between the single model that classical inference assumes and the many specifications researchers actually try. The sensitivity approaches they discuss include Rosenbaum and Rubin, Imbens, Altonji and coauthors, and Oster.

Bellemare frames robustness checks as a response to higher standards of evidence after the credibility revolution: authors “have to work hard to convince readers that their results were not cherrypicked.”

This is why papers have a robustness section. Each check should name the threat or the choice it tests. Lecture 6.5 returns to this.

Note that “robust” in “robust standard errors” is a different use of the word: those standard errors are robust to a specific feature of the errors, such as heteroskedasticity.

Example: climate and U.S. agriculture

The ideal experiment tells you which effect you are after

Question: How will a warmer climate affect U.S. agriculture?

  • Ideal experiment: give some counties a permanently warmer climate, then wait decades
  • Farmers would change crops, irrigation, and planting dates
  • The target is a long-run effect that includes this adaptation
  • A single hot year is a different experiment with a different answer

Angrist and Pischke argue that describing the ideal experiment “helps you formulate causal questions precisely.” Here it separates two effects that are easy to confuse: the effect of climate and the effect of weather.

Ask: in your proposal, what would you randomize, and how long would you wait to measure the outcome?

Three designs approximate this experiment, and each answers the six questions differently

Question Ricardian Panel weather Long differences
1. What is the relationship of interest?
2. How will you measure uncertainty?
3. Is that relationship causal?
4. What experiment would ideally measure it?
5. What variation identifies it, and what must be true of that variation?
6. What could make the identifying assumption fail?

We fill in one column at a time. Each design responds to a weakness in the one before it.

This table is the throughline for the example. The Ricardian model has an omitted-variable problem; the panel model removes it but changes the relationship estimated; long differences recover part of what the panel model loses. Each design gets two slides: the equation and the relationship of interest, then a slide that answers the remaining questions in the same order as the table.

The Ricardian model compares farmland values across counties with different climates

Vc=α+f(Tc,Pc)+Xc′γ+εc V_c = \alpha + f(T_c, P_c) + X_c'\gamma + \varepsilon_c

  • VcV_c: farmland value per acre in county cc
  • Tc,PcT_c, P_c: long-run average temperature and precipitation
  • f(⋅)f(\cdot): a quadratic in temperature and precipitation; its slope in TcT_c is the parameter of interest
  • XcX_c: soil quality and other county characteristics

1. Relationship of interest: the long-run effect of climate on land values, including adaptation (Mendelsohn et al. 1994)

The logic: land markets capitalize the expected profit from farming in a given climate, so land values already reflect how farmers have adapted.

The Ricardian assumption fails if anything that varies with climate is omitted, and irrigation does

  • 2. Uncertainty: neighboring counties share soils, markets, and climate, so their errors are correlated
  • 3. Causal? Yes, if the identifying assumption holds
  • 4. Experiment it approximates: counties assigned different climates long ago
  • 5. Variation and assumption: differences in climate across counties; no omitted factor varies with climate and affects land values
  • 6. What could make it fail: irrigated farms in drier, hotter counties draw water from elsewhere and have higher land values; Schlenker, Hanemann, and Fisher re-estimate the model for dryland counties (Schlenker et al. 2005)

Irrigation is Bellemare’s “unobserved heterogeneity” threat, and the response is a design choice: change the sample so the omitted factor no longer varies.

Discussion: which variable in your proposal could play the role of irrigation? Expect answers such as access to markets, local policy, or management practices that vary across space with the treatment.

Transition: any fixed county characteristic like irrigation access could be removed by comparing each county with itself. That is the next design.

The panel model removes fixed county differences by comparing each county with itself across years

yct=g(Wct)+γc+δt+εct y_{ct} = g(W_{ct}) + \gamma_c + \delta_t + \varepsilon_{ct}

  • ycty_{ct}: log crop yield or farm profit in county cc, year tt
  • WctW_{ct}: growing-season weather, such as degree days and precipitation
  • g(⋅)g(\cdot): the relationship between weather and the outcome; its slope is the parameter of interest
  • γc\gamma_c: county fixed effects; δt\delta_t: year fixed effects

1. Relationship of interest: the short-run response of yields or profits to weather (Deschênes and Greenstone 2007; Schlenker and Roberts 2009)

Deschênes and Greenstone use farm profits; Schlenker and Roberts use yields and allow temperature effects to be nonlinear. Both rely on the same variation.

Fixed effects solve the irrigation problem and change the relationship estimated

  • 2. Uncertainty: weather shocks hit neighboring counties together, so their errors are correlated
  • 3. Causal? Yes, for the effect of weather, if the identifying assumption holds
  • 4. Experiment it approximates: a randomly assigned hot or cool year
  • 5. Variation and assumption: year-to-year deviations in weather within a county; those deviations are as good as random
  • 6. What could make it fail: measurement error in gridded weather, and price changes that spill over across counties (a later slide covers all four)

Farmers cannot fully adapt to one hot year, so the long-run effect can be larger or smaller than this estimate (Timmins and Schlenker 2009).

County fixed effects remove everything fixed about a county, including irrigation access and long-run climate. What is left is weather.

Timmins and Schlenker give both directions. Long-run responses face fewer constraints, so long-run damages can be smaller. But some short-run responses cannot be sustained; their example is groundwater pumping, which works for one dry year and not for a permanently drier climate.

The point for the paper: gaining credibility also changed the relationship estimated, and the section has to say which one it estimates.

Transition: to recover adaptation without going back to a cross-section of levels, compare slow changes instead of yearly shocks.

Long differences compare slow changes in climate over decades

Δy‾c=αs+βΔW‾c+Δε‾c \Delta \bar{y}_c = \alpha_s + \beta\, \Delta \bar{W}_c + \Delta \bar{\varepsilon}_c

  • Δy‾c\Delta \bar{y}_c: change in county cc’s multi-year average outcome between two periods
  • ΔW‾c\Delta \bar{W}_c: change in its multi-year average weather over the same periods
  • αs\alpha_s: state fixed effects; β\beta: the parameter of interest

1. Relationship of interest: the response to gradual climate change, including adaptation (Burke and Emerick 2016)

Burke and Emerick use recent temperature and precipitation trends.

Long differences recover the adaptation that the panel model misses

  • 2. Uncertainty: one observation per county leaves far fewer observations than the panel, and errors are still correlated across neighboring counties
  • 3. Causal? Yes, for the effect of gradual change, if the identifying assumption holds
  • 4. Experiment it approximates: a climate that warms slowly over decades
  • 5. Variation and assumption: differences in climate trends across counties within a state; those trends are unrelated to other trends in outcomes
  • 6. What could make it fail: other trends, such as technology adoption, that move with climate trends

Comparing the long-differences estimate with the panel estimate for the same weather variable shows how much farmers adapted over the period. That comparison is only possible because the two designs estimate different relationships.

Your empirical strategy section answers these six questions for your design, in prose. It does not need a table like this one; the slide on writing with comments shows how to keep the questions in front of you while drafting.

In the panel weather model, each threat has an answer

Threat In the panel weather model
Reverse causality Farm output does not change the weather
Unobserved heterogeneity County effects absorb fixed differences; year effects absorb national shocks
Measurement error Gridded weather misses farm conditions; check a second weather data set
Spillovers Year effects absorb national price changes, so the estimate excludes them

This applies the four threats to one design. Each row needs an answer: the threat does not apply, the design handles it, or a robustness check tests it.

The four threats are Bellemare’s list (Section 5.2): reverse causality, unobserved heterogeneity, measurement error, and spillovers. Dalheimer and Bellemare’s Section 2.2 (shown earlier) walks through the first three in this order, and the generic version is in the identification strategy section.

Classical measurement error in weather attenuates the estimate toward zero.

The record on this design shows why data checks matter: Fisher, Hanemann, Roberts, and Schlenker (2012) identified errors in the weather and climate data used by Deschênes and Greenstone, and Deschênes and Greenstone (2012) replied with corrected estimates.

Writing the section

Put the six questions in your draft as comments, then answer each in prose

\section{Empirical Strategy}

\subsection{Estimation Strategy}
% What is the relationship of interest?
%   equation, every term defined, parameter of interest
% How will you measure uncertainty?
%   standard errors, clustering level, and why

\subsection{Identification Strategy}
% Is that relationship causal?
% What experiment would ideally measure it?
% What variation identifies it, and what must be true of that variation?
% What could make the identifying assumption fail?
%   each threat, and the design choice or robustness check that handles it

Lines that start with % do not appear in the compiled PDF. In Word, use comments; in Quarto or Markdown, use <!-- -->.

The comments keep the six questions in front of you while you draft, and a coauthor can check that each one has an answer. Delete them before submission or leave them; they do not print. This follows Bellemare’s split: inference belongs in the estimation strategy, and the threats belong in the identification strategy.

Writing task: your estimating equation and identifying assumption

For your own proposal:

  1. Write the estimating equation
  2. Define every term, including subscripts and the unit of observation
  3. Name the parameter of interest and say whether it is causal
  4. State the identifying assumption in one sentence

10 minutes. This covers checklist rows 1 to 4 and 7. Students whose proposal is descriptive should still write the equation and state what the parameter describes.
